\section{Conceptual Model}\label{conceptual-model}

\textbf{Ontological postulate:} We postulate that the physical universe is a continuous, tensioned three-dimensional brane embedded in a higher-dimensional space. This brane is ontically real; all observable phenomena—particles, forces, spacetime structure—arise from its internal oscillations and patterns of strain. The fourth embedding coordinate represents amplitude deformations of the brane, not an independent traversable dimension. Time is not a geometric coordinate but an external evolution parameter.

Key features of this ontology:
\begin{itemize}
\item The brane is spatially isotropic in its ground state; Lorentz invariance emerges as an effective symmetry from isotropic wave propagation, not as a fundamental property.
\item Amplitude deformations couple to lateral contraction, producing curvature-like effects that mimic gravity. In the weak-field limit, these deformations induce an effective metric tensor $g_{\mu\nu}$ whose Newtonian gravitational potential is sourced by the coarse-grained elastic energy density via a Poisson-like equation.
\item Particles correspond to self-confined solitonic excitations whose topology and internal phase determine charge, spin, and mass.
\item Quantization and collapse-like phenomena arise as nonlinear threshold effects, not stochastic postulates.
\end{itemize}

This section introduces (i) the microscopic substrate model and (ii) the
analytical \emph{layered} interpretation framework that connects substrate waves
to effective gauge structure.
The microscopic equations of motion are always those of the brane embedding
$\mathbf X(x,t)$; all higher-layer objects (spectral envelopes, Berry
connections, effective gauge potentials, etc.) are defined as \emph{diagnostic}
functionals of $\mathbf X$ and $\partial_t\mathbf X$ and never modify the
substrate dynamics (see also Section~\ref{experimental-setting}).

\paragraph{Layered model (overview).}
We distinguish the following layers, which will be referenced throughout the
paper:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Substrate layer:} a tensioned 3D brane embedded in $\mathbb R^4$
with time as an evolution parameter and geometric amplitude--lateral coupling.
\item \textbf{Space--time spectral layer:} local Fourier analysis producing
$(\mathbf k,\omega)$-resolved complex mode amplitudes for a \emph{real} substrate.
\item \textbf{Berry layer:} a $U(1)$ (or, at degeneracies, $U(N)$) connection on
the bundle of \emph{band-isolated} polarization eigenvectors.
\item \textbf{Electron micro-structure:} localized topological/geometry-driven
defects (W\&vdM torus + double-loop knot) that act as robust sources of
degeneracy/holonomy.
\item \textbf{Electromagnetism (effective):} an emergent four-potential and
antisymmetric field tensor identified with (a pullback of) Berry connection and
curvature.
\item \textbf{Electron (effective):} Dirac/spinor structure as the long-scale
envelope description associated with a defect-driven non-Abelian holonomy.
\end{enumerate}

The Michelson–Morley null result is naturally explained by Lorentz contraction of matter itself, rendering motion through the medium undetectable. In this view, the observed constancy of light speed reflects a dynamical property of the medium rather than a postulate about spacetime geometry.

\subsection{Continuous Formulation: A 3D Brane Embedded in 4D}\label{continuous-formulation}

Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^3$ be material (intrinsic) coordinates $x=(x^1,x^2,x^3)$.
The embedding map
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^4,\qquad
(x,t)\mapsto \mathbf{X}(x,t)=(X^1,X^2,X^3,X^4)
\label{eq:embedding-map}
\end{equation}
gives the location of each material point in 4D. Time $t$ is an external evolution parameter. The brane is \textbf{intrinsically 3D}, and its internal geometry is determined by the \textbf{induced metric}
\begin{equation}
g_{ij}(x,t)=\partial_i\mathbf{X}\cdot\partial_j\mathbf{X}\quad (i,j=1,2,3),
\label{eq:induced-metric}
\end{equation}
with reference (unstressed) metric $g^0_{ij}=\delta_{ij}$. The single unit normal $\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{R}^4$ is defined by $\mathbf{n}\cdot\partial_i\mathbf{X}=0$ and $|\mathbf{n}|=1$. The second fundamental form (extrinsic curvature) is $b_{ij}=\partial_i\partial_j\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{n}$.

\paragraph{Gauge choice and index conventions.}
The fundamental degree of freedom is the full embedding
$\mathbf X(x,t)\in\mathbb R^4$ with four real components. In analytical
derivations we sometimes introduce convenient coordinate choices (``gauges'')
to simplify expressions in the small-slope regime, but no physical restriction
to purely normal motion is assumed in the model.
Latin indices $i,j$ refer to brane (material) coordinates $1,2,3$, and time
derivatives are written explicitly as $\partial_t$.

\paragraph{Normal displacement and amplitude.}
We fix a reference embedding
\[
  \mathbf X_0(x) = (x^1,x^2,x^3,0)\in\mathbb R^4
\]
and write the full configuration as
\[
  \mathbf X(x,t) = \mathbf X_0(x) + \boldsymbol\xi(x,t).
\]
With $\mathbf n(x,t)$ the unit normal to the brane we decompose
\[
  \boldsymbol\xi(x,t)
  = \boldsymbol\xi_\parallel(x,t) + \xi_\perp(x,t)\,\mathbf n(x,t),
\]
where $\boldsymbol\xi_\parallel$ is tangential and
$\xi_\perp$ is the normal component. Throughout the paper
we use "amplitude" to refer to this normal displacement
$\xi_\perp$; in the gauge where the reference normal points
along the fourth embedding direction, $\xi_\perp\approx X^4$
in the small-slope regime.

By construction, all components of the embedding $\mathbf X=(X^1,X^2,X^3,X^4)$
carry the same physical unit of length. In particular, the amplitude coordinate
$X^4$ (and hence $\xi_\perp$) is not a dimensionless field but a genuine
geometric displacement with units of meters, on the same microscopic length
scale as the lateral coordinates $X^1,X^2,X^3$. There is therefore no separate
"amplitude unit": any change in the overall scale of normal displacements is
implemented by changing the mechanical brane parameters (mass density, tension,
bending stiffness), not by rescaling $X^4$ independently.

The \textbf{strain tensor} measuring in-brane deformation is the Green–Lagrange strain
\begin{equation}
E_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}(g_{ij} - g^0_{ij}) = \frac{1}{2}(g_{ij} - \delta_{ij}),
\label{eq:strain-tensor}
\end{equation}
which vanishes in the flat reference state and is positive (negative) for local stretching (compression). In the linear small-strain regime, $E_{ij}\approx \tfrac12(\partial_i\xi_j+\partial_j\xi_i)$ reduces to the linearized engineering strain. The stress response in this regime is
\begin{equation}
\sigma^{ij} \approx T\, g^{ij} + 2\mu\, E^{ij} + O(E^2),
\label{eq:linear-stress-strain}
\end{equation}
where $E^{ij}=g^{ik}g^{j\ell}E_{k\ell}$ denotes the raised-index strain, $T$ is the isotropic tension, and $\mu$ the shear modulus.

\subsubsection{Lagrangian and "geometric coupling"}\label{lagrangian-geometric-coupling}

We postulate a minimal elastic Lagrangian density (per unit material volume)
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
=\frac{\rho_m}{2}|\partial_t\mathbf{X}|^2
-\frac{T}{2}\mathrm{tr}(E)
-\mu|E|^2
-\frac{\kappa}{2}|b|^2,
\label{eq:brane-lagrangian}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Action and equations of motion.}
The fundamental dynamics follow from Hamilton's principle $\delta S = 0$ with
\begin{equation}
S[\mathbf X] \;=\; \int dt \int_{\Omega} d^3x\,
\mathcal{L}\big(\mathbf X,\partial_t\mathbf X,\partial_i\mathbf X,
\partial_i\partial_j\mathbf X\big).
\label{eq:brane-action}
\end{equation}
Because the bending term involves second spatial derivatives of the embedding,
the Euler--Lagrange equations take the higher-derivative form
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial X^A}
-\partial_t\!\left(\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_t X^A)}\right)
-\partial_i\!\left(\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_i X^A)}\right)
+\partial_i\partial_j\!\left(\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_i\partial_j X^A)}\right)
=0,
\label{eq:brane-eom-general}
\end{equation}
where $A=1,\dots,4$ labels the embedding directions and $i,j=1,2,3$ are brane
coordinates. These equations are second order in time but up to fourth order in
space, as in standard plate and shell theories. In the small-strain limit the
Lagrangian reduces to Eq.~\eqref{eq:linearized-lagrangian}, and the
Euler--Lagrange equations reproduce the linear wave equation discussed below.

\paragraph{Remark (substrate time vs.\ emergent spacetime).}
We treat $t$ as an external evolution parameter, not as a fourth geometric
coordinate of a covariant world-volume. The microscopic action
\eqref{eq:brane-action} is therefore not required to be four-dimensionally
diffeomorphism invariant; instead, effective Lorentz symmetry and relativistic
spacetime structure emerge from the isotropic wave dynamics in the linear
regime, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:emergent-relativity}.

where
\begin{itemize}
\item $E=\tfrac{1}{2}(g-g^0)$ is the Green strain on the brane (intrinsic deformation),
\item $T>0$ sets isotropic tension, $\mu\ge0$ adds shear stiffness (optional but useful for numerical conditioning),
\item $\kappa\ge0$ penalizes curvature (bending) via $|b|^2=b_{ij}b^{ij}$,
\item $\rho_m$ is the mass density of the brane substrate.
\end{itemize}
(Note: $|E|^2$ and $|b|^2$ denote metric contractions $E_{ij}E^{ij}$ and $b_{ij}b^{ij}$ respectively.)

\textbf{Key point:} There is \textbf{no extra field}; "amplitude–lateral coupling" arises \textbf{geometrically} because \textbf{normal (4th-direction) displacements change the induced metric $g$}, which in turn changes in-brane distances and therefore produces lateral contraction around 4D bulges.

\paragraph{Sources of nonlinearity.}

Even if the elastic law is Hookean in the Green strain, the induced metric $g_{ij}$,
the strain tensor $E_{ij}$ and the curvature tensor $b_{ij}$
are nonlinear functionals of the embedding $X^A(x^\mu)$.
Large slopes and large curvatures therefore produce nonlinear
behavior purely from the geometry of the brane.
A compact quantitative estimate of when this geometric nonlinearity
becomes important for photon-like modes is given in
Sec.~\ref{subsec:geom-threshold}, where a discrete spring-chain
analogue leads to a dimensionless parameter
$\eta \sim \tfrac{1}{2}(kA)^2$ whose order-unity value marks the
breakdown of the linear regime.

In the present paper we restrict attention to this purely geometric nonlinearity
and do not introduce any additional material saturation terms; all nonlinearity
considered here arises from the geometric dependence of $g_{ij}$, $E_{ij}$, and
$b_{ij}$ on the embedding $X^A$.

\subsubsection{Equations of motion}\label{equations-of-motion}

Variation of the action $S=\int\mathcal{L}\,dx\,dt$ gives
\begin{equation}
\rho_m\partial_{tt}\mathbf{X} = \nabla_i\left(\sigma^{ij}\partial_j\mathbf{X}\right)
-\kappa\Delta_g^2\mathbf{X}_\perp,
\label{eq:equations-of-motion}
\end{equation}
where $\nabla_i$ is the material covariant derivative built from $g$, $\Delta_g$ the Laplace–Beltrami operator (we use $\Delta_g^2=\Delta_g(\Delta_g\,\cdot)$, the biharmonic operator, applied to the normal component), $\mathbf{X}_\perp=(\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}$ the normal part, and $\sigma^{ij}$ the (symmetric) 2nd Piola–Kirchhoff stress derived from
\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial g_{ij}}
=-\frac{T}{4}g^{ij}-\mu E^{ij}.
\]
Here $E^{ij}=g^{ik}g^{j\ell}E_{k\ell}$ denotes the raised-index strain. (Exact closed forms are standard in thin-shell/branes; we keep the compact tensor form for clarity.)

\subsubsection{Linear wave regime and isotropy}\label{linear-wave-regime-and-isotropy}

Linearize about a flat embedding $\mathbf X_0(x)=(x^1,x^2,x^3,0)$ with small displacement $\boldsymbol\xi(x,t)$ so $\mathbf X=\mathbf X_0+\boldsymbol\xi$, and retain leading order. Then $g_{ij}\approx\delta_{ij}$, $E\approx \tfrac12(\partial_i\xi_j+\partial_j\xi_i)$. With $\mu\to 0$ and $\kappa\to 0$, one obtains a linear wave equation of the form
\begin{equation}
\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol\xi = c^2\Delta\boldsymbol\xi,
\label{eq:linear-wave-equation}
\end{equation}
where the effective wave speed is
\begin{equation}
c^2 = \frac{T}{\rho_m}.
\end{equation}
Throughout this work we identify this emergent wave speed with the physical speed of light $c$.

All \textbf{four components} of $\boldsymbol\xi$ (including the 4th coordinate) obey the same \textbf{isotropic} wave equation in the material coordinates. The wave speed is the same for all directions and for all embedding coordinates. Geometric nonlinearities (large slopes and curvature) introduce deviations from this ideal isotropic behavior.

\paragraph{Explicit derivation:} In the homogeneous, small-strain limit, the Lagrangian reduces to
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}\approx \frac{\rho_m}{2}|\partial_t\boldsymbol\xi|^2
-\frac{T}{2}\delta^{ij}\partial_i\xi^a\partial_j\xi^a,
\label{eq:linearized-lagrangian}
\end{equation}
and the Euler–Lagrange equations $\partial_t\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}}\right) - \delta_{ij}\partial_i\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_j\boldsymbol\xi)}\right)=0$ yield
\[
\rho_m\partial_{tt}\xi^a = T\,\delta^{ij}\partial_i\partial_j\xi^a = T\,\Delta\xi^a,
\]
which is precisely the linear wave equation $\partial_{tt}\xi^a=c^2\Delta\xi^a$ with $c=\sqrt{T/\rho_m}$.

The relativistic interpretation of this linear wave regime, and its relation to
effective Lorentz symmetry, is developed in more detail in
Section~\ref{emergent-relativity-and-lorentz-invariance}.

\textbf{Regime of validity (boxed):}
The linear wave equation~\eqref{eq:linear-wave-equation} with isotropic
dispersion is valid in the small-strain regime, where geometric nonlinearities
(curvature, threshold effects) can be neglected. In this regime the
wave speed $c=\sqrt{T/\rho_m}$ is approximately constant and independent of
direction. This linear, isotropic propagation behaviour underlies the emergent
Lorentz symmetry discussed in
Section~\ref{emergent-relativity-and-lorentz-invariance}.

\subsubsection{Geometric nonlinearity and localization threshold}\label{subsec:geom-threshold}

Define the local energy density
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}=\frac{\rho_m}{2}|\partial_t\mathbf{X}|^2
+\frac{T}{2}\mathrm{tr}(E)
+\mu|E|^2
+\frac{\kappa}{2}|b|^2.
\label{eq:energy-density}
\end{equation}
Soliton nucleation occurs when coarse-grained energy within a Compton-scale material volume reaches the electron rest energy:
\begin{equation}
\big\langle \mathcal{E} \big\rangle_{V_c}V_c \ge m_e c^2,\qquad
V_c=\Big(\frac{\lambda_C}{2\pi}\Big)^3.
\label{eq:threshold-criterion}
\end{equation}
Above threshold, the geometric nonlinearity inherent in the full
strain and curvature tensors can naturally lock in localized
high-curvature 4D bulges, turning a propagating photon-like mode
into a self-confined, particle-like soliton.

As a concrete example of this geometric nonlinearity, Appendix~\ref{app:bulge-curvature} shows that a Gaussian bulge of amplitude $A$ and width $\sigma$ in the fourth direction induces a three-dimensional scalar curvature at its center that scales as
\begin{equation}
R_3(0) \propto \frac{A^2}{\sigma^4}.
\end{equation}
This illustrates how high local curvature (or, equivalently, large amplitude gradients on Compton scales) can act as a geometric threshold for localization.

\subsubsection{"Gravity" as effective metric}\label{gravity-as-effective-metric}

Internal signal propagation uses the instantaneous induced metric $g_{ij}=\partial_i\mathbf X\cdot\partial_j\mathbf X$. Local 4D bulges (nonzero normal displacement) modify $g$, creating \textbf{geodesic focusing} for in-brane rays. We therefore identify \textbf{effective gravity} with curvature of $g$ induced by the embedding deformation. In practice we extract $g$ from simulation snapshots and ray-trace null-like paths in $(t,x)$ with interval
\begin{equation}
ds^2 = -c^2 dt^2 + g_{ij}(x,t)\,dx^i\, dx^j \quad\text{(with $x^i$ intrinsic/material coordinates)}.
\label{eq:effective-metric-interval}
\end{equation}

For numerical experiments we work on finite brane patches
$\Omega$ with physically motivated boundary conditions
(periodic, free, or clamped; see Sec.~\ref{experimental-setting}).

\subsection{From substrate waves to an emergent $U(1)$ gauge structure}
\label{sec:brane-to-berry-to-em}

The central analytical step in the new plan is:
\emph{a real-valued substrate can still generate a physically meaningful local
$U(1)$ structure}, but not by interpreting a raw FFT phase as ``the'' gauge
phase. Instead, the relevant $U(1)$ lives on the \emph{bundle of polarization
eigenvectors after band isolation} \cite{Berry1984}.

\subsubsection{Space--time spectral representation (real substrate $\Rightarrow$ complex spectra)}
\label{sec:spectral-layer}

Let $\boldsymbol\xi(x,t)=\mathbf X(x,t)-\mathbf X_0(x)$ be the displacement
around a slowly varying reference state $\mathbf X_0$. Even if
$\boldsymbol\xi(x,t)\in\mathbb R^4$ is purely real, its space--time Fourier
representation is complex:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\xi}^A(\mathbf k,\omega)
=\int_{\mathbb R}\!dt\int_{\Omega}\!d^3x\;
\xi^A(\mathbf x,t)\,e^{-i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-\omega t)},
\qquad A=1,\dots,4.
\label{eq:xi-fourier}
\end{equation}
Reality implies the usual conjugacy constraint
$\hat{\xi}^A(-\mathbf k,-\omega)=\hat{\xi}^A(\mathbf k,\omega)^\ast$.
Importantly, this does \emph{not} produce a ``3D phase vector'' at each point in
space; the complex phase of $\hat{\xi}$ is representation-dependent and is not
the geometric phase we will use.

\subsubsection{Band isolation and polarization eigenvectors (Route 2)}
\label{sec:band-isolation-route2}

Assume that on the analysis scale the substrate is locally close to a
translation-invariant configuration so that linearization yields a $k$-space
eigenproblem. For each wavevector $\mathbf k$, define a (model-dependent)
dynamical matrix $D(\mathbf k)$ such that polarization eigenvectors
$u_n(\mathbf k)\in\mathbb C^4$ and eigenfrequencies $\omega_n(\mathbf k)$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
D(\mathbf k)\,u_n(\mathbf k)=\omega_n(\mathbf k)^2\,u_n(\mathbf k),
\qquad
\langle u_n(\mathbf k),u_n(\mathbf k)\rangle = 1.
\label{eq:dynamical-matrix-eigs}
\end{equation}
The precise inner product depends on the kinetic term (mass metric); in the
simplest case it is the standard Hermitian inner product.

\paragraph{$U(1)$ despite $u_n\in\mathbb C^4$.}
Although $u_n(\mathbf k)$ has four complex components, an \emph{isolated} band is
a one-dimensional complex line: the physically relevant object is the ray
$[u_n(\mathbf k)]$ in projective space, and the only gauge freedom is the
overall phase
$u_n(\mathbf k)\mapsto e^{i\chi(\mathbf k)}u_n(\mathbf k)$.
This is exactly the single $U(1)$ that will seed the effective electromagnetic
description.

In practice we will isolate a target band $n$ by projecting the measured spectrum
onto $u_n(\mathbf k)$ (details in Section~\ref{experimental-setting}).

\subsubsection{Berry connection, curvature, and the meaning of ``curl''}
\label{sec:berry-connection-curvature}

For an isolated band, the Berry connection in $\mathbf k$-space is the $U(1)$
one-form
\begin{equation}
\mathcal A_i^{(n)}(\mathbf k)
= i\,\langle u_n(\mathbf k),\partial_{k_i}u_n(\mathbf k)\rangle,
\qquad i=1,2,3,
\label{eq:berry-connection}
\end{equation}
with curvature (the ``curl'' of the connection)
\begin{equation}
\mathcal F_{ij}^{(n)}(\mathbf k)
=\partial_{k_i}\mathcal A_j^{(n)}-\partial_{k_j}\mathcal A_i^{(n)}.
\label{eq:berry-curvature}
\end{equation}
The curvature is gauge-invariant under $u_n\mapsto e^{i\chi}u_n$.
Non-zero $\mathcal F_{ij}$ requires that $u_n(\mathbf k)$ cannot be chosen as a
globally smooth phase choice across the relevant region of $\mathbf k$-space,
which typically happens when the band structure contains singularities
(degeneracies, defects) or when symmetries that would force trivial topology are
broken. These conditions become testable numerically once the band is identified.

\subsubsection{Pullback to space--time and identification with an effective four-potential}
\label{sec:berry-pullback-em}

To connect the $\mathbf k$-space Berry structure to an effective field on
space--time, consider a wave packet with slowly varying local wavevector
$\mathbf k=\mathbf k(x,t)$ (estimated from the band-projected complex envelope).
The Berry phase accumulated along a path is
$\gamma=\int \mathcal A_i(\mathbf k)\,dk_i$.
This defines a pulled-back one-form on space--time:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal A_\mu(x,t)
=\mathcal A_i(\mathbf k(x,t))\,\partial_\mu k_i(x,t),
\qquad \mu=0,1,2,3,
\label{eq:berry-pullback}
\end{equation}
with $x^\mu=(ct,x^1,x^2,x^3)$ and $\partial_0\equiv \tfrac{1}{c}\partial_t$.

We then \emph{define} an effective electromagnetic four-potential by a single
conversion factor (to be fixed by matching to the electron charge in the
effective theory),
\begin{equation}
A_\mu^{\mathrm{EM}}(x,t)=\frac{\hbar}{q}\,\mathcal A_\mu(x,t),
\label{eq:em-potential-from-berry}
\end{equation}
and the corresponding antisymmetric field tensor
\begin{equation}
F_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{EM}}
=\partial_\mu A_\nu^{\mathrm{EM}}-\partial_\nu A_\mu^{\mathrm{EM}}.
\label{eq:em-tensor-from-berry}
\end{equation}
By construction, $F_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{EM}}$ satisfies the Bianchi identity
$\partial_{[\lambda}F_{\mu\nu]}^{\mathrm{EM}}=0$, which corresponds to the
homogeneous Maxwell equations. The inhomogeneous equations (dynamics and
sources) become a concrete empirical question: they must arise as the effective
long-wavelength dynamics of the band-projected envelope coupled to defects.

\subsubsection{Degeneracies, defects, and the non-Abelian generalization}
\label{sec:nonabelian-berry-electron}

When a relevant mode subspace is \emph{degenerate} (dimension $N>1$), the correct
geometric structure is the Wilczek--Zee non-Abelian connection
$\mathcal A_i^{ab}=i\langle u_a,\partial_{k_i}u_b\rangle$
with gauge group $U(N)$ \cite{WilczekZee1984}.
In the present programme, robust localized defects (the W\&vdM-inspired toroidal
electron micro-structure) are expected to act as precisely such degeneracy
sources: the ``frame'' of polarization eigenvectors becomes ill-defined at the
defect core, and holonomy around it can encode spinor-like behaviour (including
$4\pi$ periodicity) at the effective layer. This supplies a concrete mechanism
for how the description can transition from an Abelian (electromagnetic) sector
to an electron sector that requires a non-Abelian geometric phase.
